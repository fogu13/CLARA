# Chapter 3: Methodology

## 3.1 Research Paradigm: Design Science Research

This thesis adopts Design Science Research (DSR), the paradigm whose contribution is a purposeful artifact built for a relevant organisational problem and the design knowledge it yields (Hevner, March, Park and Ram, 2004). The paradigm fits because the aim is constructive: the problem, feedback collected but not acted on, is documented (Forrester, 2025b; Fazio et al., 2025; Homburg and Fürst, 2005; Wirtz et al., 2010), and the open question is how to design a system that closes the loop under governance.

The work follows Hevner's (2007) three-cycle view. The relevance cycle connects the artifact to the practitioner problem (Chapter 1), the requirements of VoC practice and the EU regulatory context, and a practitioner evaluation that is designed (§3.6) but was not conducted at the time of writing; the rigour cycle grounds the design in Chapter 2; the design cycle iterates build-and-evaluate until the artifact is fit for evaluation.

![Figure 3.1: Design Science Research: Hevner's three cycles and the DSRM activities, instantiated for this thesis.](../diagrams/rendered/08_dsr_method.png)

The activities follow the Design Science Research Methodology (DSRM) of Peffers, Tuunanen, Rothenberger and Chatterjee (2007):

| DSRM activity | Instantiation in this thesis |
|---|---|
| 1. Problem identification and motivation | The feedback-to-action gap (Chapters 1 and 2): collection without operationalisation, unmeasured closure, lost memory. |
| 2. Objectives of a solution | A governed Signal → Insight → Action → Learning loop; contracted, graded closure; perishable, retrievable memory; EU AI Act and GDPR-aligned oversight (§1.3; Chapter 4). |
| 3. Design and development | The artifact and its design decisions (Chapter 4). |
| 4. Demonstration | The worked example on the demonstration dataset (§4.8) and the gold-set pipeline run on the 188-signal corpus (§5A). |
| 5. Evaluation | Bounded agreement with reference labels (§5A); the practitioner study with SUS and TAM (§5B) was not conducted at the time of writing. |
| 6. Communication | This thesis and the defence. |

Following Gregor and Hevner (2013), the intended contribution is an improvement: an instantiation and nascent design theory (Chapter 6).

## 3.2 Research Questions and the Working Hypotheses

The four research questions (§1.3) are the claims this thesis answers, and §1.7 states the evidence status behind each. Eight working hypotheses formulated at proposal stage (H1–H8) are subsumed by the questions; they survive as coding labels in the interview guide and survey (Appendix A) and as rows of the traceability matrix (Appendix D), each carrying one of the evidence statuses of §1.7. They are design propositions assessed for plausibility, not statistical effects tested for significance.

RQ3b is not derived from the working hypotheses; the build-evaluate cycle is expected to surface questions the design cycle did not pose (Hevner, 2007). It arose from the observation that the rule-based floor escalated none of the eight German-source signals whose reference label warranted escalation (§5A.5), and it is answered descriptively by that evidence. Because the corpus texts are English paraphrases and source language is confounded with sector (§3.5.4), RQ3b concerns source strata, not German-text processing.

## 3.3 Research Design Overview: Mixed Methods

The evaluation was designed as a convergent mixed-methods study: a quantitative gold-set evaluation of how far the artifact's enrichment reproduces reference sentiment and risk labels on paraphrased public feedback, and whether escalation recall differs across source strata (RQ3a, RQ3b; §3.5), and a practitioner study of semi-structured interviews and task-based prototype sessions with SUS and TAM (RQ4; §3.6).

The design is reported as it stands. The quantitative stream was run and carries the thesis's measured (bounded) claims. The design claims (RQ1, RQ2) rest on an implemented and regression-tested architecture and a worked demonstration (Chapter 4), with the documented regulatory interpretation of Appendix B. The practitioner study was not conducted at the time of writing. Its protocol, instruments and pre-registered reporting tiers are retained (§3.6, Appendix A, §5B), but RQ4 has no empirical answer here, and the practitioner side of RQ2, whether people understand the controls, catch errors and accept their cost, is likewise not conducted.

## 3.4 Artifact Development Method

The artifact was built in iterative build-evaluate cycles, dated in Appendix G.8. Deterministic responsibilities (schema validation, rule conflict resolution, audit logging, action execution, outcome scoring) are implemented in code; bounded language-model reasoning sits behind a single provider-agnostic interface, so that model behaviour is isolated and testable. Before any practitioner session the platform is to be placed under a feature freeze, permitting only fixes the evaluation requires. Its telemetry stream (candidate acceptance, approvals, executions, measurement checkpoints, loop verdicts; §4.6 states what it does not record) is designed to feed, with facilitator observation, the task metrics of the protocol (§5B.2; Appendix A).

## 3.5 Quantitative Evaluation Design (RQ3a, RQ3b)

### 3.5.1 Gold-set construction

The quantitative evaluation treats the enrichment and routing pipeline as a multi-field classifier and measures agreement with reference labels ("seeds") attached to a corpus of 188 paraphrased, de-identified public reviews (§3.7). Two gold standards are used, and neither gold standard is human-labelled: sentiment is scored against the customer's own star rating, a human-origin proxy that rates the review rather than annotating the paraphrased text, and risk against assistant-authored seed labels (§3.7). The sentiment gold covers the 153 star-rated signals and the risk gold the 106 risk-labelled signals; journey stage and owner are scored on 182 signals under a constrained closed-set configuration (§5A). Theme and action carry seed labels but no metric (§3.5.2).

A second reference set lives inside the artifact: the curated golden set that drives the in-repo evaluation harness, a development and validation benchmark co-developed with the artifact and not independent confirmation (§5A.7). At the published GLM-5.2 snapshot of 6 September 2026 it holds 100 items (72 EN / 28 DE) with a held-out split of 30. Its German stratum is authored, not naturally occurring, because no natural German customer texts were available, so its German figures characterise constructed German. The set's growth, labelling rules and audit are recorded in Appendix G.2 and G.4.

### 3.5.2 Metrics

The predicted fields differ in cardinality, and the cardinality dictates the metric.

- Categorical fields. Risk has a closed four-level label set; journey stage and owner carry open-vocabulary seeds (27 and 51 distinct labels) and are scored under the constrained closed-set configuration of §3.5.1, in which the inventory is supplied. All three are scored with accuracy (with a 95% Wilson interval), macro-F1 and per-class confusion matrices. Risk is also collapsed to a binary escalate-or-not decision (high or critical against the rest), because whether a signal reaches a human at all is the property the loop depends on.
- Open-vocabulary fields. `theme_seed` and, to a degree, `recommended_action_seed` are free text, so exact match is an unfairly strict metric. The designed procedure is semantic agreement (predicted and gold labels embedded and compared by cosine similarity against a threshold, with a clustering-agreement measure and a small adjudication of borderline cases to calibrate the threshold). It was designed and was not executed for this thesis (§5A.1); no theme or action metric is reported.
- Sentiment is scored against `star_rating_1_5` where present, a human-origin proxy independent of the assistant that drafted the seeds, which bounds circularity for sentiment only (§3.5.4).

One harness metric, the hallucination heuristic, token-grounds predicted tags against the English tag vocabulary and therefore omits non-English items rather than flag correct German tags as hallucinated; the model card discloses the restriction.

### 3.5.3 Breakdowns and baselines

Results are reported per sector (fintech, B2B industrial, food delivery) and per source stratum (English-source, German-source; the `language` field records the source review's language, while the texts are English, §3.7). This is the bounded test of H3 and a descriptive probe of the concern that feedback from some origins is triaged less reliably (Mehrabi et al., 2021). Stratum results are always reported with their denominators, never as a blended average.

Five predictors are compared on identical items: a rule-based floor (a bilingual lexicon classifier for sentiment, a keyword classifier for risk), TF-IDF + logistic regression scored by five-fold cross-validation, the generic prompt on GLM-5.2, and the production stage on GLM-5.2 and on Mistral Small 4 (the production default); journey stage and owner are compared with majority-class floors (§5A.2). Predictors are compared pairwise with an exact McNemar test on the discordant items, in one retrospective family per task of the ten unique comparisons among the five predictors, corrected by Holm step-down (m = 10; §5A.3–5A.4, §5A.6). No comparison was declared primary before the runs, so every contrast is exploratory in the statistical sense: the Holm-adjusted exact test decides, the paired difference with its Wald interval is a descriptive effect size, an interval that excludes zero on a non-surviving row is not evidence of a difference, and intervals on fewer than ten discordant pairs are approximate. A non-significant difference is reported as not distinguishable on this corpus, never as equivalence. The harness (`evaluation/`) writes every table metric and figure from data; the few hand-assembled prose figures are marked in §5A.5.

For the few-shot exemplar store (Chapter 4), the designed inference is an in-run paired A/B: within one run every golden-set item is scored with exemplars off and on, and the paired difference is tested with McNemar's test, because the models are non-deterministic even at temperature 0 and cross-run comparisons confound an intervention with model noise. Its results are exploratory in the statistical sense and rest on contemporaneous records whose per-item records were not preserved: the growing-set p-value trajectory is sequential testing without alpha spending, same-day re-runs of identical items do not substitute for alpha control, and the held-out split enters the pooled safety veto, so it is a safety-validation set rather than an untouched test set (§5A.7; Appendices G.3 and G.4).

### 3.5.4 Threats to validity

The reference labels were drafted by a language-model assistant alongside the datasets and used as delivered (§3.7), so they are a documented reference, not an oracle; for risk no verified human check exists, and the second rating of §3.7 is reported as second-rating agreement with independent human provenance unverified. Two corpus properties bound the stratum results. Per-language slices are source-language strata, say nothing about German-text processing, and are confounded with sector: the German-source risk-labelled signals are almost entirely B2B industrial (38 of 45) and the English-source ones overwhelmingly fintech (58 of 59). And because every text passed through paraphrasing, part of the lexicon floor's failure may reflect the paraphrase style, so that finding is claimed for paraphrased operational phrasing (§5A.3). The corpus is 188 signals assembled to be balanced rather than representative, so accuracy figures characterise the pipeline on this corpus, not a population. Using a language model both to author the risk seeds and to predict risk introduces a same-family bias of unknown direction; the star-rating check bounds circularity for sentiment only, and the theme adjudication of §3.5.2 was not executed, so no theme claim is made. The estimator of §3.5.5 has run on simulated outcome data only.

### 3.5.5 Quasi-experimental outcome scoring: interrupted time series

Whether a remediation action worked cannot be established by a randomised experiment in the single-organisation pilot deployments considered here: the action reaches every affected customer at once. Naive before/after deltas confound the intervention with trends and seasonality, and recovery actions can have null or negative effects (Halperin et al., 2022), so measurement is default-on. The platform therefore scores outcomes with an interrupted time series (ITS) design using segmented regression (Wagner, Soumerai, Zhang and Ross-Degnan, 2002; Bernal, Cummins and Gasparrini, 2017), the standard quasi-experimental design for an intervention with a known onset and no concurrent control.

Closure has three meanings, and the platform keeps them apart. Delivery means the approved action left the system, evidenced by an execution record with the connector's external reference. Target attainment means an instrumented reading from the window or follow-up checkpoint, bound to its own plan, met the contracted threshold over its frozen interval; the loop verdict `loop_closed` is issued on attainment, and the T+7 early read can report on track but never a verdict. Attributed improvement means a fitted interrupted time series on the problem detail, graded C only when the segmented-regression fit was obtained, and it is not a validated causal design. Two measurements serve these meanings:

| Measurement | Estimand | Window | Grade | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Scheduled checkpoint | Signal rate over a fixed interval versus the contracted baseline | Interval frozen when the checkpoint was scheduled (T+7, window close, follow-up) | D (E if manual) | Decides target attainment; the window or follow-up checkpoint decides the loop verdict |
| Detail-view ITS estimate | Model-implied difference at the window end between the fitted post-intervention trend and the pre-intervention counterfactual | Complete UTC days from the plan origin, intervention day excluded | C when fitted, D when refused | Read-time estimate shown beside the checkpoint; never the verdict |

**Model.** For a problem theme with journey-stage-scoped daily signal counts $y_t$ (signals per complete UTC day), the intervention falls at day $t_0$. What $t_0$ is read from is recorded on the measurement plan as its origin: the dispatch instant of a successful push (the moment the approved action left the system); the human-recorded implementation instant, when someone attests that the fix landed (an attestation the system does not verify), which supersedes the pending checkpoints and restarts the clock; or the approval instant only when the draft itself is the deliverable because no connector is configured, a case the readout labels as running from approval. A failed push starts no clock, because nothing reached the world that inflow could react to. The model is

$$y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \beta_2\,\text{post}_t + \beta_3\,(t - t_0)\,\text{post}_t + \varepsilon_t$$

where $\text{post}_t = \mathbb{1}[t \ge t_0]$; $\beta_2$ estimates the immediate level change and $\beta_3$ the slope change. The reported effect is the model-implied difference at the end of the observation window between the fitted post-intervention trend and the pre-intervention counterfactual, $\hat\Delta = \beta_2 + h\beta_3$ with $h$ the horizon in days, with a 95% confidence interval from the OLS covariance of $\beta_2$ and $\beta_3$ and Student-$t$ critical values at $n-4$ degrees of freedom. A fitted model is not a validated causal design. The estimate remains exposed to seasonality, autocorrelation and time-varying confounding (Bernal et al., 2017); its intervals are ordinary OLS intervals pending heteroskedasticity-and-autocorrelation-consistent errors; the fit is OLS on rates rather than a count model; and when actions on one theme overlap, the earliest execution is attributed (§6.4).

**Series construction (bias controls).** Three rules, each addressing a bias identified during development: complete UTC days only, because the read-time day is a partial observation that drags the endpoint of the fit (simulation on a no-effect series showed spurious effects in mid-day reads); the day of $t_0$ excluded, because it mixes pre- and post-intervention exposure and assigning it to the post segment understates $\beta_2$ and manufactures a spurious $\beta_3$; and no fabricated history, so the pre-window (up to 28 days before the origin) is truncated at the first observed signal rather than padded with zeros.

**Minimum data.** The estimator refuses below its minimum data requirements (10 pre-days, 5 post-days) and reports a labelled plain difference of means ("insufficient data for ITS") with no confidence interval. In the implemented and tested build, outcome series derive only from ingested signals and the simulation harness cannot write measurements; the estimator's only runs to date were on simulated series fabricated outside that path (§3.5.4), so its benefit on live outcome data is untested.

**Outcome contracts.** The measurement is contracted before acting. At approval time the system proposes a contract that is applied by default, editable and declinable: the baseline is the trailing signal rate over the observed span (capped at 28 days), with a 30-day measurement window, a T+7 early read and a success threshold of a 50% rate reduction. Contracts are versioned: the proposal at approval and every later amendment create a new revision; the primary metric cannot be changed once an observation has been recorded against it; each checkpoint is scheduled with the complete terms frozen on the plan and is scored under those terms over a fixed observation interval set when it was scheduled, so checkpoints are scored on intervals frozen when they were scheduled; every reading freezes the terms it was scored under, so a reading already on record keeps the status it earned under the revision in force when it was taken and an amendment never re-labels it; an amendment supersedes the pending checkpoints explicitly and re-plans them under the new revision on the same clock (recorded as `measurement_replanned`); and a legacy checkpoint without frozen terms is blocked rather than scored under terms nobody scheduled it with.

**Grading and bounding the measurement itself.** Three design elements make the quality of each measurement legible. First, every outcome readout is stamped with an evidence grade (A–E) that grades the measurement design as realised, not the result; the grade is read from an explicit table of methods, never inferred from a method's name (Appendix D; the correction from name-inference to the explicit table is logged in Appendix G.7): A (randomised holdout) and B (controlled quasi-experiment) are reserved in the taxonomy and not produced by the platform, so a contract naming either is graded as what its data support, D; C is an interrupted time series only where the segmented-regression fit was obtained; D is every uncontrolled before/after read, which is what a checkpoint reading is; and E is a manual assertion, an unrecognised method or a reading of unknown provenance. The grade combines the comparison method with the measurement provenance: every measurement is either instrumented (scheduler-computed from signals) or manual, and manual values are labelled "unverified manual observation". The checkpoint reading carries the grade of its own design (a rate comparison over its fixed observation interval: D); the problem detail additionally runs the live ITS fit and grades that estimate on the fit obtained, so an ITS contract whose series was too sparse to fit reads D and never C, and a `loop_closed` readout always states that attribution is not established. Second, declared guardrail metrics are measured at every checkpoint. The repeat-signal guardrail (the daily rate of post-execution theme signals from customers already observed in the 28-day pre-window, omitting identity-less signals) is a worsening-threshold check against that baseline (the code's design note calls it a non-inferiority check; no statistical non-inferiority test is performed): a relative worsening above 20% informs but never blocks. Third, at contract proposal time a detectability note applies a two-sample Poisson normal-approximation minimum-detectable-effect heuristic at 80% power and flags windows too small to detect the contracted effect; it is labelled a heuristic, not a power analysis (Cohen, 1988), and it is a lower bound, because the two-sample bound ignores the variance the estimator adds through the slope term. For the default contract (28 pre-days, 30 post-days, a 50% reduction) the break-even baseline is about 37.6 signals per day per theme under the estimator's own variance, against 2.2 under the two-sample bound (`thesis/review-2026-09-04-recompute.py`, `its_detectability`); below that rate the contract is a directional read, and the note says so.

These mechanisms are implemented and regression-tested in the current build; §4.1 dates what was deployed. The design-to-code mapping is given in Appendix D.

## 3.6 Qualitative Evaluation Design (RQ4)

The practitioner study is a planned protocol. No session had been run at the time of writing; §5B.1 reports its status and §5B.3 the pre-registered rule for reporting whatever sample it reaches.

### 3.6.1 Participants and sampling

The target population is marketing, product and customer-experience practitioners at small and mid-sized companies that actively collect customer feedback. Purposive sampling seeks variation across the three roles and across company size. The recruitment target is 12–15 completed interviews (Appendix A, consent and recruitment), over-booked to absorb no-shows, recruited via LinkedIn, practitioner communities and referral chaining.

### 3.6.2 Procedure

Each session is a short online semi-structured interview in two parts: the participant's current practice, how their organisation acts on feedback today and where it stalls (H1, H2, H5, H8), and a task-based encounter with the prototype in four tasks (T1, triage a batch of signals to the most urgent problem; T2, decide who owns a theme and whether its actions need one approver or two; T3, approve a queued action; T4, inspect a closed-loop measurement and its learning record; §5B.2, Appendix A). Task completion and time-to-action are to be captured by facilitator observation and by the platform's `events` telemetry.

### 3.6.3 Instruments

After the task portion, participants are to complete the System Usability Scale (Brooke, 1996) and a short Technology Acceptance Model battery measuring perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use (Davis, 1989), plus author-written trust and friction items on auditability and human oversight (H6, H7). The instruments are reproduced in Appendix A.

### 3.6.4 Analysis

Transcripts are to be analysed by codebook thematic analysis, following the six phases of Braun and Clarke (2006) with a structured codebook (Appendix A). Braun and Clarke (2019, 2021a, 2021b) distinguish codebook approaches, in which a codebook is fixed early by consensus and agreement between coders is a meaningful quality check, from reflexive thematic analysis, in which themes are constructed late and no inter-coder statistic belongs to the method. The planned analysis starts from deductive codes derived from H1–H8, adds inductive codes, fixes the codebook by consensus before the full coding pass, and double-codes a subset for agreement; no reflexive-specific validity claim is made. The second coder is a role to be filled, not a named person: a practitioner or researcher who took no part in the interviews, briefed on the codebook only. Double-coding is planned, not done. The agreement statistic is Cohen's κ on code presence per transcript segment over a double-coded subset of at least one quarter of the transcripts; κ below 0.60 triggers a codebook revision and re-coding before coding proceeds (Appendix A, codebook). SUS is to be scored conventionally and reported as a mean with a t-based 95% confidence interval against the published anchors (Bangor, Kortum and Miller, 2009; Sauro and Lewis, 2016). The tiers of §5B.3 govern which summaries may be reported at the sample the study reaches.

## 3.7 Datasets

The evaluation uses only publicly sourced customer-feedback datasets; synthetic datasets were excluded. Three datasets are used, chosen for sector and source-language diversity:

| Dataset | Sector | Qual. signals | Dominant source language | Gold labels |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Trade Republic | Fintech / digital brokerage | 67 | EN | theme, journey stage, owner, action, risk |
| Henkel (adhesives) | B2B industrial / consumer goods | 39 | DE | theme, journey stage, owner, action, risk |
| Lieferando | Food delivery | 82 | DE / EN | theme, journey stage, owner, action, company-response pattern (no risk label) |
| Total | | 188 | EN 102 · DE 84 · other 2 | |

The text is paraphrased rather than quoted and de-identified (names, usernames, order numbers and direct personal details removed), with source URLs and collection dates retained for provenance. The paraphrased texts are English throughout, and the `language` field records the language of the source review, so the corpus is multilingual in origin but not in text; per-language results in §5A are source-language strata, and the golden set's authored German items are the only German-language texts in the evaluation (§3.5.1; the history of its held-out split is in Appendix G.3). Each signal carries seed labels and, where available, a 1–5 star rating. The Lieferando set carries a company-response-pattern label instead of a risk label, so every risk figure in §5A rests on the 106 risk-labelled Trade Republic and Henkel signals, and every sentiment figure on the 153 star-rated signals.

The corpus was collected, paraphrased and labelled in an assistant-led research session and has not been independently human-adjudicated. Customer star ratings provide a human-origin sentiment proxy; the remaining seeds are assistant judgements. A second rating of 40 risk-labelled items agrees with the seeds at κ = 0.55 (0.38–0.72) unweighted and 0.70 (0.56–0.82) linear-weighted, but its independent human provenance is unverified, so it is reported as second-rating agreement with independent human provenance unverified and is not used as human validation. Appendix G.1 documents the provenance and the agreement statistics.

The rating file is `evaluation/results/kappa_risk.json`. The seed labels were produced without reference to the artifact, but they are unreviewed assistant judgements, and the risk, journey-stage and owner comparisons inherit the same-family bias that §3.5.4 names. Public pages expose only sampled subsets, the corpora are balanced rather than representative, customer claims are unverified, and B2B industrial feedback is sparse in public sources, so the Henkel set leans on consumer-grade product reviews; these constraints bound external validity (Chapter 6).

## 3.8 Evaluation Criteria

Following the taxonomy of IS-artifact evaluation of Prat, Comyn-Wattiau and Akoka (2015), the artifact is assessed against four criteria: efficacy and accuracy (agreement with the reference labels, §5A); perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use (the TAM constructs of the practitioner study, §5B, not conducted at the time of writing); fidelity to the problem (the worked demonstration of §4.8); and Responsible-AI properties, that is, human oversight, auditability and regulatory alignment, assessed by a documented interpretation against EU AI Act and GDPR requirements (Appendix B) and, when the study runs, by participants' trust ratings.

## 3.9 Research Ethics

The planned practitioner study involves human participants and personal data and is to be conducted under the OPIT ethics route for RAI-9001, which the University's programme contact confirmed in August 2026 covers this study's interviews and anonymous survey; no separate approval number was issued. Participation is voluntary, with the right to skip questions, stop at any time and withdraw data within a stated window. Informed consent (Appendix A) covers purpose, recording and transcription, anonymisation, storage and retention, the lawful basis (consent) and access or erasure. No special-category data is sought. The evaluation datasets are public, paraphrased and de-identified, and are processed only for academic research.

## 3.10 Limitations, Threats to Validity, and Positionality

Three limitations are intrinsic to the design. This is a design-validity study, not a controlled field experiment: it does not and cannot claim a reduction in time-to-action or a causal retention effect. The corpus is 188 labelled signals and the practitioner study was not conducted, so findings are design knowledge bounded to one corpus, not population estimates. And the researcher is also the artifact's designer, with a commercial interest in the venture it underpins (Conflict of Interest statement, front matter): the study's most significant bias risk.

Threats to validity. Construct validity: SUS and TAM measure perceived usability and perceived usefulness, not whether the loop demonstrably closes faster, which is why RQ4 is framed as design validity. Internal validity: no causal claim is made, so the main threat is the reference labels themselves, assistant-drafted and used as delivered (§3.7); the second rating of §3.7 is reported as second-rating agreement with independent human provenance unverified, no adjudication of the seeds has taken place, and a human annotation study with adjudication is an early item of the remaining evaluation work (§6.5). External validity: the corpus is three sectors and two source-language strata of English paraphrases, balanced rather than representative, so results transfer as design knowledge with the sector and stratum breakdowns marking the limits of transfer. Conclusion validity and reliability: the quantitative pipeline is fully scripted and writes every metric in its tables from data, the few prose figures assembled by hand from those tables are marked where they appear (§5A.5), and the qualitative analysis, when it runs, is a codebook analysis with planned double-coding (§3.6.4).

Positionality and safeguards. Two safeguards are in place and two are planned. In place: the sentiment gold is a star-rating proxy independent of the seed-drafting assistant and of the researcher, and the seed labels were produced without reference to the artifact (§3.7); and the quantitative analysis is reproducible, so claims can be re-derived independently, with disconfirming evidence reported, including the production stage's escalation recall of 28 of 50 under the seed labels (§5A.4). Planned for the practitioner study, if it runs: standardised subscales (SUS, and the PU and PEOU subscales of TAM), with the author-written trust and friction items labelled as such and the friction items reverse-keyed so that agreement bias cannot inflate only the favourable picture (Appendix A); and codebook analysis with double-coding (§3.6.4). Negative and null findings are reported as first-class results. These limitations are revisited in Chapter 6.
