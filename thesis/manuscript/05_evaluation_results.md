# Chapter 5: Evaluation and Results

This chapter reports the evaluation introduced in Chapter 3. Section 5A is the quantitative gold-set evaluation of the artifact's enrichment and routing pipeline on 188 paraphrased customer signals: agreement with reference labels (RQ3a) and escalation recall across source strata (RQ3b). Section 5B records the status of the practitioner study (RQ4), which was not conducted at the time of writing, together with its protocol and the reporting rule fixed in advance. Every metric in the §5A.2–5A.5 tables is computed by the reproducible harness in `evaluation/` and read from `evaluation/results/` and, for the production default, `evaluation/results_mistral-small-2603/`; the in-repo figures of §5A.7 are read from `apps/api/app/evals/published_metrics.json` or, where marked, from the author's contemporaneous record. No figure is recomputed in prose.

## 5A. Quantitative Gold-Set Evaluation (RQ3a, RQ3b)

### 5A.1 The corpus and the reference standards

The evaluation corpus is 188 English paraphrases of public customer reviews, de-identified and drawn from three sectors (`corpus_stats.csv`, `composition.csv`; collection and labelling are described in §3.7):

| Sector | Dataset | Signals |
|---|---|---:|
| Food delivery | Lieferando | 82 |
| Fintech / brokerage | Trade Republic | 67 |
| B2B industrial | Henkel (adhesives) | 39 |
| Total | | 188 |

The `language` field records the source review's language (English 102, German 84, other 2). The paraphrased texts are English throughout, so every per-language figure in this chapter is a source-language stratum, not a text-language result.

Two reference standards are scored. Sentiment is scored against the customer's star rating on the 153 star-rated signals (1–2 stars negative, 3 neutral, 4–5 positive; 94 negative, 9 neutral, 50 positive), a human-origin proxy that is independent of the text the classifier reads. Risk is scored against a closed-set seed label (low, medium, high, critical) on the 106 risk-labelled signals of the Trade Republic and Henkel datasets (36 low, 20 medium, 23 high, 27 critical), drafted with the datasets by a language-model assistant (§3.7). Neither gold standard is human-labelled: the star rating is the one human-origin reference, and the remaining seeds are assistant judgements. A second rating of 40 risk seeds agrees with them at κ = 0.55 (0.38–0.72) unweighted and 0.70 (0.56–0.82) linear-weighted (27 of 40 exact, 38 of 40 within one level; `kappa_risk.json`), but its independent human provenance is unverified, so it is reported as second-rating agreement with independent human provenance unverified and is not used as human validation (Appendix G.1). Journey stage (27 distinct values) and recommended owner (51) are scored under a supplied inventory (§5A.4.1). Theme and recommended action carry seed labels on all 188 signals but no metric, because the semantic-agreement procedure designed for open-vocabulary fields (§3.5.2) was not executed; §5A makes no accuracy claim about either.

### 5A.2 Predictors and headline results

![Figure 5.1: Real-data gold-set evaluation harness: three real datasets and their seed labels feed three predictors (rule-based, classical ML, and the LLM path), scored against the same gold labels.](../diagrams/rendered/07_evaluation_pipeline.png)

The figure shows the three predictor families of the harness on the three paraphrased datasets of §5A.1; the five predictors scored are the following, on identical items.

1. The lexicon floor: a bilingual (EN/DE) lexicon sentiment classifier and a keyword severity classifier in the pre-learning tradition (Taboada et al., 2011). No training; its German entries are largely idle on English paraphrases.
2. TF-IDF + logistic regression: character n-grams, stratified 5-fold cross-validation with out-of-fold predictions and class balancing.
3. The generic prompt: a zero-shot triage prompt on GLM-5.2 at temperature 0 (`evaluation/predict_llm.py`) asking for three-class sentiment, a four-level risk label, a theme, a journey stage and an owner. The reported run (run 0) is of 4 August 2026, 188 of 188 signals enriched; the run's provenance and a harness defect it exposed are in Appendix G.5. Three repeats were run on 6 September 2026. It is a contextual predictor on the artifact's model, not the artifact's enrichment stage.
4. The production stage on GLM-5.2: `services/enrichment.py` itself, run on the corpus on 5 September 2026 through `evaluation/predict_llm_production.py`. The production stage differs from the generic prompt in at least four ways that each move the number: four-class sentiment including a mixed label, an urgency rubric rather than a risk rubric, curated few-shot exemplars, and batching with a response sanitiser. The scoring rules were fixed before either production run: a mixed prediction maps to neutral against the three-class star gold, with a drop-mixed sensitivity reported beside it, and the urgency label is scored as risk unchanged.
5. The production stage on Mistral Small 4 (the production default): the same stage and scoring rules, run on the same day on the configured production model.

Pairwise differences are tested with the exact McNemar test on identical items. Because no comparison was declared primary before the runs, the ten unique comparisons among the five predictors on each task form one retrospective family per task, corrected by the Holm step-down (m = 10; `evaluation/multiple_comparisons.py`, which writes `results/paired_tests_holm.csv` from `mcnemar_paired.csv` in both results folders and `compare_runs_pairs.csv`). Each row also carries the paired accuracy difference, diff = (c − b) / n, with a Wald 95% interval, as a descriptive effect-size range; the Holm-adjusted exact test decides, an interval that excludes zero on a non-surviving row is not evidence of a difference, and intervals on fewer than ten discordant pairs are approximate. All comparisons are exploratory in the statistical sense. A non-significant difference is not equivalence and is reported as "not distinguishable on this corpus".

### 5A.3 Sentiment results

The floor is weak for a substantive reason: of the 94 gold-negative signals, the lexicon labels 58 neutral (`sentiment_confusion.csv`), because operational phrasing ("Transaction history cannot be exported to CSV") carries few overt sentiment words; negative-class precision is 0.94 and recall 0.34 (`sentiment_perclass.csv`). The neutral class is small (9 of 153) and depresses macro-F1 for every predictor.

Every learned or contextual predictor separates from the floor, and all four floor comparisons survive the correction. Nothing else does. The generic prompt's lead over TF-IDF + logistic regression (19 discordant pairs to 7; +0.08, interval +0.01 to +0.14) is stable across four repeated runs on the same items (b = 7 throughout, c = 19 or 20, unadjusted p = 0.019–0.029) and not significant after the retrospective correction (0.174); the interval excluding zero on that row is not evidence of a difference. The production stage's lead over TF-IDF + logistic regression on either model (+0.05 and +0.06) is not distinguishable on this corpus (0.906 and 0.610), and neither is the production stage from the generic prompt on the same model name (8 pairs to 3; 0.906) or the two production configurations from each other (3 pairs to 5; 0.906), the last two resting on fewer than ten discordant pairs. The finding that stands is that on this corpus surface-lexicon sentiment falls far below every learned or contextual predictor; which of those is better cannot be decided on 153 items.

Four comparisons survive the correction: TF-IDF + logistic regression, the generic prompt and the production default each separate from the floor, and the production stage on GLM-5.2 sits below the generic prompt on the same model name (26 pairs to 3; −0.22, interval −0.31 to −0.13). One reading is that severity in this corpus is carried substantially by lexical cues that character n-grams learn, which would explain why the contextual predictors add less here than on sentiment; the corpus does not test that reading. #### 5A.4.1 Routing fields: journey stage and recommended owner

Journey stage and owner are open-vocabulary in the production prompt, and the model's free-form vocabulary barely intersects the corpus gold by exact string, so scored that way the result would measure wording rather than routing. They are therefore scored from a constrained run (`predict_llm.py --constrained`) in which the corpus taxonomy is supplied as a closed inventory and the model selects from it, the closed-set treatment §3.5.2 designed for these two fields and the condition a configured workspace resembles. The model obeyed the inventory on every item. It is a different configuration from the runs behind §5A.3 and §5A.4 and is never merged with them. Six of the 188 signals were lost to endpoint errors, so n is 182, and both coverage and the end-to-end figure are reported. Against a majority-class floor of 0.20, journey stage reaches accuracy 0.59 and macro-F1 0.50 over 27 classes; against a floor of 0.15, owner reaches 0.40 and 0.32 over 51 classes. Restricted to the classes with at least five examples, macro-F1 is 0.55 and 0.42 with accuracy 0.58 and 0.44 on the same items (`f1_macro_on_supported`, `accuracy_on_supported` in `summary.json`; provenance in Appendix G.7). Both fields clear their floor, and neither is accurate enough to route unattended: an owner assignment that is correct two times in five would misroute the majority of problems as automation. These fields are a draft a human accepts or corrects. The supplied-inventory score is not an upper bound on free-form routing; it is a different, constrained task. The approval gate (§4.4; DP4 in §6.1) is defended on exactly this reading: it controls false positives, the wrong or harmful actions that would otherwise reach an external system; it cannot recover escalation-worthy signals the enrichment ranks too low (22 of 50 under the seed labels on the production rubric, §5A.4.2).

#### 5A.4.2 The production enrichment stage on the same corpus

The generic-prompt rows answer what the model can do on this corpus; the production rows answer what the artifact does. The first production run is on GLM-5.2, the model of the 4 August generic run, so that the two rows share a model name. They do not share a run date, and the generic prompt's own September repeats disagree with its August run on a share of risk labels (below), so the pair is a configuration contrast, prompt/pipeline × run date behind one model name at a gateway that may have changed, and not an isolated effect of the pipeline. The second is the production default, Mistral Small 4 (`mistral-small-2603`, EU-hosted); the two production runs also differ in batch size and run conditions (Appendix G.6), so that pair is a deployment-configuration comparison, model × batch size × run conditions, and not an isolated model effect. The pairs are read from `evaluation/results/compare_runs_pairs.csv` (rows `glm_generic_run0_vs_glm_production`, `glm_production_vs_mistral_production`, `ml_vs_glm_production` and `ml_vs_mistral_production`) and the accuracies from `compare_runs_accuracy.csv`. `evaluation/results/summary.json` carries paired tests for the GLM-5.2 runs only (its `mcnemar_paired` block pairs the floor, TF-IDF + logistic regression, the generic prompt and the GLM-5.2 production stage) and carries no Mistral pair, so the deployment-configuration pair is read from the CSV alone.

Sentiment. Under the same model name the production stage sits three points below the generic prompt, and the two production configurations sit within a point of each other; neither difference is distinguishable on this corpus (§5A.3), and p above 0.05 on discordant counts of 8 to 3 and 3 to 5 means the paired difference is imprecisely estimated, not that it is zero. The pre-registered mixed rule carries a visible share of the residual error. GLM-5.2 labelled 23 of the 153 star-rated signals mixed and one neutral (Mistral Small 4: 11 and 4), and the star gold has no such class (`sentiment_llm_production_raw_label_counts`). Dropping the mixed items instead of mapping them to neutral gives 0.94 (0.88–0.97, n = 130) on GLM-5.2 and 0.89 (0.83–0.93, n = 142) on Mistral Small 4 (`sentiment_llm_production_excluding_mixed`). Neither figure is the headline: the rule was fixed in advance so that the reader can see what rides on it, and what rides on it is that a model reading a review of the form "good product, poor support" (an illustration, not a corpus item) as mixed is answering a question the star rating does not ask.

Risk. The fall from the generic prompt to the production stage under the same model name (−0.22, interval −0.31 to −0.13) is the largest of the non-floor contrasts and survives the correction (§5A.4), although part of it may belong to the month between the runs. It is a systematic shift rather than noise. Read as an ordinal scale, 100 of the 106 GLM-5.2 predictions and 96 of the 106 Mistral Small 4 predictions are within one level of the seed label (`risk_llm_production_confusion.csv` and the Mistral folder): the production rubric places the corpus's critical at high and its high at medium, with every gold-low signal labelled low. Precision on critical is 1.00 on GLM-5.2 and 0.77 on Mistral Small 4, at recall 0.11 and 0.37. What the seed labels call escalation-worthy (high or critical), the production stage flags as high or critical for 28 of 50 signals on either model, an escalation recall of 0.56 against 0.88 for the generic prompt in run 0, 0.78–0.80 in its repeats and 0.90 for TF-IDF + logistic regression; on the binary escalate-or-not decision the production stage scores 0.79 (0.71–0.86) on GLM-5.2 and 0.78 on Mistral Small 4, against 0.92 for the generic prompt (`summary.json`, `risk_llm_binary_escalation` and `risk_llm_production_binary_escalation`; the Mistral folder's `summary.json`, `risk_llm_production_binary_escalation`). Two explanations fit and the corpus cannot separate them. The production prompt defines urgency (how soon someone must act) while the seed labels define risk (what is at stake), so part of the shift may be a construct difference; the scoring rule fixed before the runs scores the urgency label as risk unchanged (§5A.2), so that difference is visible in the figures rather than removed by a re-mapping. Or the exemplar store calibrates the rubric conservatively, a design choice for precision at the cost of recall. The second rating of the risk seeds points the same way, placing seven of its ten seed-critical items at high, but its independent human provenance is unverified, so it qualifies the shift without discharging the artifact of it. A one-level re-mapping would restore most of the accuracy and is not applied, because it would be a rule written after seeing the result: the production stage's severity output is an uneven capability against this gold standard and is published as such. Which explanation dominates is answerable by relabelling the risk field under the production rubric's own definition, blind to the model output, the annotation study of §6.5. For the approval gate the consequence is the one stated in §5A.4.1: it controls false positives and cannot recover the 22 of 50 escalation-worthy signals the production rubric ranks below the escalation line.

Run-to-run stability. The production default was run four times under the identical configuration and the generic prompt on GLM-5.2 four times in all (the folders are named in Appendix G.6, which carries the run histories). At temperature 0 the production default assigns identical sentiment labels on 99.3–100% of the star-rated signals and identical urgency labels on 97.2–100% of the risk-labelled signals across the four runs (`results_mistral-small-2603/compare_runs_agreement.csv`). The generic prompt is less stable and the two tasks differ: the four runs agree on 97.4–99.3% of sentiment labels and on only 85.8–94.3% of risk labels, and the split is by date, the three September runs agreeing with each other on 93.4–94.3% and with the August run on 85.8–86.8% (`results/compare_runs_agreement.csv`). Whether that is a change in the model behind the gateway or chance cannot be told from four runs. What can be said is that the generic prompt's sentiment lead over TF-IDF + logistic regression is stable across the four runs and its run-0 risk accuracy is not, and that the production stage on GLM-5.2 (0.50) scores below each of the four generic runs (0.65–0.72); the three September repeats fall within a day of the production run, so the run-date component of that contrast is smaller there than against the August run, but no paired test on those pairs is reported and the pair remains a prompt/pipeline × run date contrast. The production stage on GLM-5.2 was run once; it is not the configured production default.

For the model-sovereignty commitment of §4.4 the result is bounded. On this corpus the EU-hosted production default scores one point above the GLM-5.2 production configuration on sentiment and nine points above it on risk (6 pairs to 16; Holm 0.157; +0.09, interval +0.01 to +0.18, which is not evidence of a difference on a non-surviving row), so no accuracy cost of the commitment was measured, which is not the same as showing that there is none: the two configurations differ in model, batch size and run conditions together, and an isolated model effect needs the matched rerun scripted as `evaluation/run_matrix.py`, which had not been executed at the time of writing (Appendix G.6).

### 5A.5 Generalisation across sectors and source strata

Per-slice results are descriptive. The harness writes sentiment metrics by sector, source language and review platform (`sentiment_by_sector.csv`, `sentiment_by_language.csv`, `sentiment_by_source.csv`, and the production-stage blocks of each `summary.json`); the lexicon floor transfers unevenly across them, and the generic prompt and the production stage score above the floor in every sector slice (`sentiment_llm_by_sector`, `sentiment_llm_production_by_sector`); no per-sector slice is written for TF-IDF + logistic regression. Source, sector and language are confounded on this corpus, so no slice is reported as a source, sector or language effect. Among the 106 risk-labelled signals, the German-source stratum (45 signals, 8 escalation-worthy) is almost entirely B2B industrial (38 of 45) and the English-source stratum (59 signals, 41 escalation-worthy) overwhelmingly fintech (58 of 59); one signal is Spanish-source and one Dutch-source, the Dutch one escalation-worthy, so the stratum table counts 49 of the 50 positives. Because the texts are English paraphrases, no slice measures German-text processing, and an aggregate "German versus English" accuracy would largely restate the sector composition; no cross-language classification gap is claimed.

What can be examined is escalation. The fairness criterion is equal opportunity, the recall on signals whose seed label warrants escalation (high or critical), because it conditions on the gold label and is not distorted by the very different base rates across strata (17.8% German-source, 69.5% English-source; `escalation_recall_by_language.csv`). "Warrants escalation" here means labelled high or critical in the assistant-drafted reference, not an independently established fact. Each recall carries a 95% Wilson interval and each stratum pair a two-sided Fisher exact test on hits and misses (`escalation_recall_fisher.csv`):

| Escalation recall | German-source (8 positives) | English-source (41 positives) | Fisher p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Keyword floor | 0 of 8 (0–32%) | 8 of 41 (10–34%) | 0.32 |
| TF-IDF + logistic regression | 5 of 8 (31–86%) | 39 of 41 (84–99%) | 0.026 |
| Generic prompt, run 0 | 7 of 8 (53–98%) | 36 of 41 (74–95%) | 1.0 |
| Production stage, either model | 5 of 8 (31–86%) | 22 of 41 (39–68%) | 0.72 |

The generic prompt's three repeats reach 6 of 8 against 32–33 of 41, lower in both strata than run 0. These figures are descriptive, and an eight-item denominator makes them easy to over-read. Each Fisher test asks, for one predictor, whether its two stratum recalls differ; it says nothing about whether one predictor's gap differs from another's. That the within-predictor test of TF-IDF + logistic regression crosses 0.05 and the generic prompt's does not is not a comparison of the two gaps: the difference between a significant and a non-significant result is not itself significant (Gelman and Stern, 2006), and a test on the difference of the two differences is not reported (a paired item-level bootstrap and a permutation test are specified in `evaluation/equity_gap_bootstrap.py` and had not been run on the corpus at the time of writing). No ranking of the predictors by equity is derived, and no parity is demonstrated for any of them. A rival explanation for the observed gap of TF-IDF + logistic regression is named rather than dismissed: it is trained by cross-validation on this corpus, in which the German-source escalation cases are eight and almost all B2B vocabulary, so an in-domain classifier inherits that composition and the zero-shot predictor does not; an English-only training ablation would separate method from training composition and has not been run. The production stage's recall is below the generic prompt's in both strata (5 of 8 and 22 of 41), consistent with the one-level shift of §5A.4.2; a within-predictor Fisher test compares the two strata of one predictor and says nothing about a shortfall shared by both.

An uneven triage layer does not merely score worse; it means some customers' problems are less likely to reach a human at all, which is a property of the loop rather than of a classifier and the reason escalation is monitored per stratum (Mehrabi et al., 2021; the fairness-monitoring item of §6.5). Three limits bound the reading. The German-source stratum has eight positives, so its intervals are wide. Language remains confounded with sector on this subset. And because the texts are English paraphrases, what differs across strata is the content and register of what German-language customers wrote about, such as B2B product issues phrased without alarm keywords, not German-language processing.

### 5A.6 Interpretation and threats

The statistical reading of §5A.3 and §5A.4 is a single one. After the retrospective Holm correction, the comparisons that remain significant are, on sentiment, the four floor comparisons, and, on risk, floor → TF-IDF, floor → generic, floor → production Mistral and generic → production GLM; every other contrast, including the floor → production GLM comparison on risk (0.124), does not survive. The change from the earlier two-family analysis of six comparisons per task to the ten-member family changes one status: TF-IDF → production GLM on risk was significant under the six-member family (0.038) and is not under the ten-member one (0.064). So the finding for RQ3a is that on this corpus agreement with the reference labels is strongly method-dependent, that every learned or contextual predictor separates from the lexicon and keyword floors on these paraphrases, and that the choice among the learned and contextual predictors is not decided by this corpus: the generic prompt's sentiment lead over TF-IDF + logistic regression is stable across repeated runs on the same items and not significant after the correction, the production stage on either model is not distinguishable from TF-IDF + logistic regression on sentiment, and on risk the production stage sits below the generic prompt on the same model name, with a one-level rubric shift that lowers escalation recall to 0.56. H3, that reliability depends on signal type and method, is measured in this bounded sense. RQ3b is answered descriptively: escalation recall differs observably across source strata for some predictors on this corpus, without a language effect, a fairness ranking or evidence of parity (§5A.5).

Three limits bound the comparison. Only TF-IDF + logistic regression is cross-validated; the language-model rows rest on a reported run plus repeats, and the production stage on GLM-5.2 was run once. The seed labels are assistant-drafted and used as delivered (§5A.1, §3.7), and agreement with independent human judgement is not measured. And the corpus is modest and balanced by construction rather than population-representative, so the figures characterise the pipeline on this corpus and are not population estimates; performance on natural deployment data is open. What §5A measures is a precondition of the contribution, how far the enrichment feeding the loop agrees with reference labels on one corpus, and not the contribution itself; whether that agreement is sufficient for drafting under review is a practitioner question that §5B did not test. DP1 and DP2 are implemented and tested (Chapter 4); whether an outcome contract or a decaying memory improves real decisions is not measured here, and DP2 remains an untested hypothesis (§5A.7, §6.1). The scope is four of the six gold fields, sentiment and risk for the generic prompt and the production stage, and journey stage and owner only under a supplied taxonomy; theme and recommended action carry labels but no metric. The production stage scores 0.83 on this corpus against 99% on the artifact's own golden set on the same model and pipeline (§5A.7); the two streams are never merged because the gold standards differ, and the lower, star-proxy figure is the one to quote.

## 5B. Practitioner Study (RQ4)

### 5B.1 Status

The practitioner study was not conducted at the time of writing. No interview, task session or survey response had been collected, no finding is reported, and nothing here is simulated. The practitioner sessions planned for September 2026 are reported in this section under the rule of §5B.3 if any run before submission; otherwise RQ4 stays unanswered and the hypotheses the study was to test are marked not conducted in Appendix D. The evidence status of every RQ4 claim in this thesis is therefore "not conducted".

### 5B.2 Protocol

The protocol, instruments and recruitment materials are in Appendix A and are summarised here only so that a later report can be read against them. The protocol specifies that twelve to fifteen practitioners in marketing, product or customer-experience roles at organisations that actively collect feedback are to be recruited purposively with role and company-size variation (eligibility and session length are specified in Appendix A). Each online session is to have two parts: a semi-structured discussion of current practice (H1, H2, H5, H8) and a task-based prototype encounter with think-aloud, comprising four tasks: T1, triage to the most urgent problem; T2, decide who owns a theme and whether its actions need one approver or two; T3, approve a queued action; T4, inspect a closed-loop measurement and its learning record. Task completion and time-to-action are to be captured by the facilitator and the platform's `events` telemetry. Participants are then to complete the System Usability Scale and the technology-acceptance and trust battery (Appendix A). A short anonymous survey, one Likert item per hypothesis plus a source-count and a closure-frequency item (Appendix A; distribution described in `thesis/instruments/survey_distribution.md`), is to add breadth on the current-practice and attitude hypotheses and is analysed by `evaluation/survey_analysis.py`; it is self-report, its sample would be a convenience sample, and H6 and H7 would be measured there as hypothetical comfort rather than observed trust, so it never substitutes for the sessions.

### 5B.3 Reporting rule

The reporting rule is fixed in advance so that it cannot depend on the sample reached, and the two instruments are tiered separately. Interview and task tiers depend only on the interview count: with twelve or more interviews the protocol is executed as designed, the SUS and TAM figures are reported with intervals and the thematic findings are reported as findings; with six to eleven, the same instruments are reported with the SUS and TAM figures as descriptive ranges, the task metrics per participant and every theme labelled with its n; with fewer than six, no theme is reported as a finding, the sessions are reported as a design walkthrough with reactions attributed by role, and the hypotheses concerned are marked not conducted in Appendix D. **Survey tiers depend only on the survey n.** With n ≥ 40 on a hypothesis's own usable denominator the full descriptive analysis is reported, top-2-box agreement with Wilson intervals and the per-hypothesis verdict the script issues, where the script's CONFIRMED label is reported as "supported in this sample" and a verdict that rests on a corroborating item (H2, H8) also needs that item at n ≥ 40; with 10 ≤ n ≤ 39 descriptive counts and intervals are reported and no verdict is issued, the script writing `DESCRIPTIVE ONLY (n < 40)` in place of one (Appendix G.7) so that the results file and the manuscript cannot disagree; with n < 10 only the number of responses is reported. The survey never suppresses an interview finding and never upgrades one; a survey verdict, where issued, is reported beside the interview themes as a separate line of evidence, and both are entered in the traceability matrix (Appendix D) so that the evidence status of every claim is stated in one place. Transcripts, when they exist, are analysed by codebook thematic analysis rather than the reflexive variant, for the reasons and with the distinction stated in §3.6.4 after Braun and Clarke (2021a), against the codebook in Appendix A, with a double-coded subset, Cohen's κ under the 0.60 revision threshold and the reconciliation rule of §3.6.4, and a second coder who is a role to be filled rather than a named person. An empty result is stated in a sentence, never left as a placeholder.
