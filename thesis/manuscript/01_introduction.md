# Chapter 1: Introduction

## 1.1 Background and Motivation

Customer feedback has never been more abundant or more strategically prized. Organisations run Net Promoter Score (NPS), customer satisfaction (CSAT), and customer effort (CES) surveys; they accumulate support tickets, app-store reviews, social mentions, sales-call transcripts, and behavioural product analytics. The Voice of the Customer (VoC), a structured process for capturing customer needs and translating them into product and service decisions, was formalised three decades ago (Griffin & Hauser, 1993) and has since evolved from episodic market research into an always-on organisational capability spanning the multi-touchpoint customer journey that Lemon and Verhoef (2016) conceptualise. The promise is simple: listen continuously, learn what customers need, and act.

The reality is more sobering. The binding constraint is no longer the *collection* of feedback but its *operationalisation*, the translation of insight into action. Forrester's 2025 survey series on feedback management finds close-the-loop practice promising but ineffective in most organisations, and the communication of feedback to the people who could act on it too slow to drive action (Forrester, 2025b, 2025c); the barriers it reports are structural rather than analytical: unclear ownership, and the absence of a workflow from feedback to the responsible team. Soliciting feedback is not neutral either: Bone et al. (2017) show in two field experiments that how a firm asks for feedback changes what customers subsequently spend, so feedback that is collected and then not acted on is a liability rather than an inert record. Wirtz, Tambyah, and Mattila (2010) show that much customer feedback reaches frontline employees informally and goes unreported to the organisation, so the loop leaks before it starts. And Ghasemaghaei, Hassanein, and Turel (2017) show that analytics capability translates into organisational agility only when the tools fit the tasks and the people who use them, not by virtue of data quality alone. In short, the problem is not that organisations cannot see what customers are saying. Seeing rarely becomes doing, doing is rarely measured, and what is learned is rarely remembered.

Industry evidence through 2025 and 2026 corroborates the academic picture and sharpens its urgency. Forrester's 2025 Customer Experience Index recorded US CX quality at an all-time low, with 25% of brands' rankings declining versus only 7% improving for a second consecutive year, and its 2025 Voice-of-the-Customer survey found that most CX teams still cannot get stakeholders to act on insights, with only 27% communicating insights in a timely way (Forrester, 2025a, 2025b; Doerer & Wassel, 2025). Analyst and practitioner reporting converges on a single diagnosis: customer-experience programmes have "an action problem". Insight is cheap, and execution is what is missing. The economic backdrop is a fast-growing, increasingly AI-mediated Voice-of-the-Customer software market, sized at roughly USD 9.5 billion in 2025 with forecast compound annual growth of 15–17% (Custom Market Insights, 2025; QKS Group, 2025). The cost of leaving feedback unacted-upon is therefore rising even as the tooling proliferates.

Three developments make this a good moment to revisit the problem with a design-oriented lens. First, large language models (LLMs) have made the enrichment of unstructured feedback (sentiment, theme, urgency, and evidence extraction) cheap, multilingual, and good enough to operationalise, which lowers the cost of the *insight* stage that earlier VoC systems struggled with. Second, the same capability raises a governance question. If AI can not only interpret feedback but recommend and even execute responses, then human oversight, auditability, and regulatory compliance under the EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) and the GDPR become design requirements, not afterthoughts. Third, the competitive landscape has moved: leading feedback-intelligence vendors now market "action" and "agentic" capabilities, yet they largely stop at detection and routing and do not prove that the customer's problem was actually resolved (analysed in Chapters 2 and 6). Together these create a clear opening for a system that closes the loop *and* governs the closing.

## 1.2 Problem Statement

This thesis addresses four interlocking deficiencies that together constitute the **feedback-to-action gap**:

1. **Signal fragmentation.** Customer signals arrive across many disconnected sources (surveys, support systems, reviews, social channels, behavioural analytics) and are rarely unified into a single prioritised view, so it is hard to know which problems matter most (Neslin et al., 2006; Verhoef, Kannan, & Inman, 2015).
2. **The insight-action gap.** Even when a problem is identified, organisations lack structured, governed mechanisms to decide *what action is warranted*, route it to the right owner, and execute it through the systems they already use (Bone et al., 2017; Ghasemaghaei et al., 2017).
3. **Unmeasured closure.** Actions, when taken, are seldom tied to an explicit outcome contract, so organisations cannot tell whether the loop actually closed, that is, whether the action *resolved* the underlying problem rather than merely producing a ticket. This matters because well-intentioned responses are not automatically value-creating. In a natural field experiment with roughly 1.5 million customers, apologies after service failures did not restore future spending on their own, and repeated or promise-laden apologies *reduced* it (Halperin, Ho, List, & Muir, 2022). Acting on feedback without measuring the outcome can make things worse.
4. **Lost organisational memory.** What worked, in which context, is rarely codified. Hard-won learnings depreciate and are forgotten, and organisations relearn the same lessons repeatedly (Walsh & Ungson, 1991; Argote, 2013).

No single tool in the current market addresses all four end-to-end. Enterprise VoC suites excel at collection. Product-feedback tools excel at "what to build". Emerging feedback-intelligence platforms excel at analysis and, increasingly, at alerting and routing. But the chain from a raw signal through a *measured* outcome and a *reusable* learning, under explicit governance, is owned by no one (Chapter 6 develops this competitive analysis in detail).

The thesis can be stated as a single claim: **closing the feedback loop is a workflow-and-governance problem, and the two primitives the market is missing are a *measured outcome contract* and a *perishable learning memory*, not a better dashboard.** Everything that follows designs, builds, and tests around those two primitives.

## 1.3 Research Aim and Questions

The aim of this thesis is to **design, build, and evaluate a software artifact that operationalises the complete feedback-to-action loop (Signal → Insight → Action → Learning) with Responsible-AI governance embedded throughout, and to derive transferable design principles from doing so.**

This aim is pursued through four research questions:

- **RQ1 (core).** How can a software artifact operationalise the full feedback-to-action loop such that actions are not merely *recommended* but *governed, executed, and designed so that their closure is measured*, with reusable organisational learning captured along the way?
- **RQ2 (governance).** What design principles enable *governed* action-taking (every action a draft behind a mandatory human approval gate, authority graduated by consequence class, and an audit trail) that aligns with the EU AI Act and GDPR, and how do practitioners experience the friction that governance imposes?
- **RQ3a (accuracy).** How accurately does the artifact's AI enrichment pipeline reproduce human-assigned sentiment, risk/severity, journey-stage and owner labels on real-world customer feedback, and how does accuracy vary across sectors?
- **RQ3b (equity).** Does the choice of triage method change *whose* problems get escalated? That is, does escalation of genuinely critical signals differ systematically across language strata, defined in this corpus by the source review's language (§3.7)?
- **RQ4 (qualitative).** How do practitioners assess the artifact's usefulness, usability, and trustworthiness for closing the feedback-to-action gap?

RQ1 and RQ2 are answered through the design and demonstration of the artifact (Chapter 4) and reflective design-principle synthesis (Chapter 6). RQ2 is additionally answered by a documented design walkthrough against the EU AI Act and GDPR (Appendix B), evidence that does not depend on practitioner data, with the friction clause answered by practitioner perception (§5B). RQ3a and RQ3b are answered through a quantitative gold-set evaluation against real, human-labelled feedback (Chapter 5, §5A): RQ3a by per-field accuracy, RQ3b by escalation recall stratified by source language (§5A.5). RQ4 is answered through a qualitative practitioner study, combining semi-structured interviews and task-based prototype sessions with a System Usability Scale (SUS) and Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) instrument (Chapter 5, §5B).

The scope of RQ3a is deliberately narrower than the pipeline as a whole. The pipeline also assigns theme and a recommended action. Those fields carry gold labels but are **not** scored, because the semantic-agreement procedure they require (§3.5.2) was not executed. Journey stage and owner *are* scored, but only under a supplied taxonomy rather than in the production free-form configuration (§5A.4.1), which makes those figures an upper bound on production routing accuracy. Extending the evaluation to them is stated as future work (§6.5), and no accuracy claim is made about them anywhere.

Eight working hypotheses guided the early project; they are subsumed by these questions and are retained only as coding labels in the practitioner instruments and in the traceability matrix (Appendix D), which also records what each claim's evidence status is.

## 1.4 The Artifact in Brief

The artifact is a working web platform that instantiates the Signal → Insight → Action → Learning cycle as a pipeline of bounded stages. Signals are ingested, enriched by a language model, synthesised into insights, accepted by a person as problems, proposed as actions that a person approves before anything leaves the system, measured against an outcome contract, and codified as a learning whose confidence decays over time (the stages are detailed in Chapter 4). Responsible-AI governance is a property of the loop itself, not a separate module: human approval of every action, policy checks that can withhold it, an audit trail with a hashed evidence pack, a published model card, and model sovereignty (the option to run on a self-hosted, EU-resident model behind a residency gate).

The platform deliberately *integrates with* rather than replaces the tools organisations already use to collect feedback (surveys, support systems) and to act (issue trackers, CRMs, marketing systems). Its claim to contribution is not a better dashboard but the *governed path* from a raw signal to a measured outcome and a codified learning, which is the stretch of the chain where the literature locates the gap.

The system is implemented as **CLARA** (Next.js front end, FastAPI/LangGraph backend; detailed in Chapter 4). The design was first instantiated on a different stack, a React/Vite single-page application on Supabase, which served as the first design-cycle iteration and has been superseded (§4.7). The thesis contribution is independent of implementation stack.

## 1.5 Research Approach

The thesis adopts **Design Science Research** (DSR), the paradigm appropriate to research whose primary output is a purposeful artifact that addresses a real organisational problem (Hevner, March, Park, & Ram, 2004; Peffers, Tuunanen, Rothenberger, & Chatterjee, 2007). DSR legitimises the dual contribution pursued here: a *situated instantiation* (the working platform) and *prescriptive design knowledge* (transferable design principles) (Gregor & Hevner, 2013). The artifact is evaluated through a mixed-methods strategy that combines a quantitative, gold-set evaluation of the enrichment pipeline on real labelled data with a qualitative practitioner study, triangulating objective accuracy with practitioner-perceived utility and trust. The full methodology, including the rationale for design-validity rather than statistical-effect claims, is set out in Chapter 3.

## 1.6 Contributions

This thesis makes four contributions:

1. **A working artifact** that closes the customer feedback-to-action loop end-to-end, from signal to *measured* outcome and *reusable* learning, under explicit governance. It demonstrates that the gap identified in the literature is addressable by design.
2. **Transferable design principles**, derived from non-trivial design decisions that the literature does not resolve: closure as a contracted, graded measurement that refuses when underpowered; perishable organisational memory via confidence decay; authority graduated by consequence class with every action a draft; a bounded model inside a deterministic loop; and the decision to measure, publish and not ship a capability whose errors fall unevenly on the people served (Chapter 4; synthesised as five principles and two implementation decisions, each with its cost, in Chapter 6).
3. **A real-data evaluation** of LLM-based feedback enrichment and routing against human-curated labels across three sectors (fintech, B2B industrial, food delivery), with signals drawn from English- and German-language sources and scored as English paraphrases (§3.7), quantifying where automated triage is reliable and where it is not (Chapter 5, §5A).
4. **An account of integral Responsible-AI design**: how human oversight, auditability, and EU AI Act / GDPR alignment can be built into an action-taking AI system as durable design commitments rather than compliance afterthoughts, with reflections on the tensions this surfaces (Chapters 4 and 6).

## 1.8 Scope and Delimitations

The thesis scope is the *governed loop-closure* stretch of the chain. It is explicitly **not** a survey-collection tool, a full customer data platform, a campaign-delivery engine, or a product-management suite. These are treated as systems to integrate with, not to rebuild. The quantitative evaluation assesses the enrichment and routing pipeline against human labels on a corpus of real public feedback; it does not claim production-scale benchmarking. The qualitative study evaluates *perceived* utility, usability, and trust through practitioner interviews and task sessions; it does not measure long-run business outcomes such as retention lift, which would require a longitudinal field deployment beyond the thesis timeframe. The platform pushes approved actions to real external systems (Jira, Slack) and pulls feedback through official-API connectors (Zendesk, Trustpilot, the Apple App Store, Google Play, Google Business Profile) alongside CSV import and a signed webhook; CRM-side integrations remain future work, judged unnecessary for evaluating the design concept (Chapter 4, §4.7).

## 1.9 Responsible AI Positioning

As an MSc Responsible AI thesis, this work treats Responsible AI as an **integral, evaluated design dimension** rather than the headline contribution. The headline is the governed closing of the loop. The Responsible-AI commitment is what makes that closing *defensible* when the system can recommend and execute consequential actions. Concretely, this means human oversight aligned with EU AI Act Article 14, transparency and logging consistent with Articles 50 and 12, GDPR data-governance principles, and honest trust calibration in the interface (Lee & See, 2004). Fairness is treated as an *evaluated* property rather than an implemented control: the evaluation measures whether triage escalates critical signals evenly across languages (§5A.5, RQ3b), while automated routing-fairness enforcement is design-forward and not built (§6.4), and standing fairness monitoring is stated as future work (§6.5) (Mehrabi et al., 2021). An earlier framing of the project as an ethics-governance "guardian" for marketing journeys was deliberately set aside, on the supervisor's guidance, in favour of the orchestration-and-closure framing adopted here. The Responsible-AI substance of that earlier direction is retained as a cross-cutting design concern.

## 1.10 Structure of the Thesis

**Chapter 2** reviews the literature underpinning the artifact: VoC and feedback loops, sentiment analysis and NLP, business-intelligence pipelines, anomaly detection, decision support and automation, experimentation, churn, data integration, visualisation, workflow management, and organisational learning, together with the Design Science Research methodology, the Responsible-AI literature, and recent (2023–2026) developments in LLM agents, agentic feedback platforms, and EU AI Act implementation. **Chapter 3** sets out the research methodology: the DSR paradigm and its activities, the mixed-methods evaluation design, the datasets, and the research ethics. **Chapter 4** documents the artifact, its architecture, the five design decisions that constitute the design contribution, and its Responsible-AI controls. **Chapter 5** presents the evaluation: the quantitative gold-set results (§5A) and the qualitative practitioner study protocol and findings (§5B). **Chapter 6** discusses the design principles, the Responsible-AI tensions, limitations, and future work, and positions the artifact against the competitive landscape. **Chapter 7** concludes.
