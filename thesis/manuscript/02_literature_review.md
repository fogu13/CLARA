# Chapter 2: Literature Review

This chapter reviews what prior work settles, and what it leaves open, about four design problems that a governed feedback-to-action system must solve: turning feedback into accountable action (§2.1), enriching feedback when the labels available to test the enrichment are themselves imperfect (§2.2), interpreting the evidence that an action worked while governing automated action (§2.3), and reusing what was learned under conditions that change (§2.4). Two foundations follow, the design-science paradigm (§2.5) and the Responsible-AI and EU-law setting (§2.6), and §2.7 states the gap the thesis addresses together with the limits of the review's coverage. The review is narrative rather than systematic: sources were located through the academic literature and the vendor and analyst documentation listed in the reference list, with the access dates it records, and not through a protocol-driven database search. It can therefore show what was found and cannot establish that nothing else exists.

## 2.1 Turning feedback into accountable action

### 2.1.1 What is collected and what it measures

Griffin and Hauser (1993) formalised Voice of the Customer as a structured process for capturing customer needs and translating them into product and service attributes, and Gaskin et al. (2010) updated its methods. Blazevic et al. (2013) extended collection to online engagement as a continuous rather than episodic source, and Lemon and Verhoef (2016) placed feedback within a multi-touchpoint journey, so that VoC became an ongoing organisational capability rather than a periodic study. The consequence is always-on capture across many instruments at once (NPS, CSAT, CES, support tickets, reviews, behavioural data), which is the volume that motivates automated triage.

Which instrument to trust has been argued for two decades. Reichheld (2003) proposed the Net Promoter Score as a single predictor of growth; Keiningham et al. (2007) and Morgan and Rego (2006) found it no better, and often worse, than satisfaction measures at predicting firm performance; De Haan, Verhoef and Wiesel (2015) showed across industries that no single metric predicts retention best everywhere and that combining metrics improves prediction. Dixon, Freeman and Toman (2010) added customer effort as a predictor of loyalty that satisfaction does not capture, and both SERVQUAL (Parasuraman, Zeithaml and Berry, 1988; critiqued by Cronin and Taylor, 1992) and the American Customer Satisfaction Index (Fornell et al., 1996; Anderson, Fornell and Lehmann, 1994) model satisfaction as a construct with several measured causes. The settled position is multi-metric: a feedback system should carry several converging measures rather than elect one. Kumar, Pozza and Ganesh (2013) add that the satisfaction–loyalty link itself varies by context, which cautions against reading any collected score as if it were an outcome.

### 2.1.2 The feedback-to-action gap

The gap between collecting feedback and acting on it is documented from two sides. Inside the organisation, Wirtz, Tambyah and Mattila (2010) found that unsolicited feedback received by service employees largely stays with them, a social-capital problem in reporting rather than a measurement problem at the edge. Homburg and Fürst (2005) showed that organisational complaint handling drives loyalty through perceived justice, and that a mechanistic approach (guidelines, defined process, assigned responsibility) does so more strongly than an organic, culture-led one, which locates the remedy in process design. On the practitioner side, Forrester's 2025 survey of feedback-management and CX-measurement programmes finds that most still struggle to get stakeholders to act on insights and suffer from a lack of stakeholder confidence (Forrester, 2025b), and its close-the-loop report finds the practice promising but less effective than it could be, with slow communication of feedback and unclear ownership among the reasons (Fazio et al., 2025). Bone et al. (2017) contribute a narrower, experimental point: soliciting open-ended positive feedback changes customers' subsequent purchasing, so the act of asking is itself an intervention. Their experiments concern solicitation, not organisational routing, and they are not evidence about which mechanisms firms lack.

Read together, these sources support a structural reading of the gap. What the process studies and the practitioner surveys describe as missing is not analysis but the mechanisms that carry a signal onward: an accountable owner, a record of what was done, and a path across functional boundaries. That reading frames the artifact of Chapter 4 and separates a feedback-analysis problem from a feedback-operations one. Whether the artifact's mechanisms change what practitioners do is a question this thesis leaves open (§1.7).

### 2.1.3 Fragmentation and integration across sources

Multichannel customer management identified data integration as its primary operational challenge (Neslin et al., 2006), and omni-channel management defines the goal as the synergetic management of channels and touchpoints rather than their parallel operation (Verhoef, Kannan and Inman, 2015). Homburg, Jozić and Kuehnl (2017) conceptualise customer experience management as a firm-level capability, with cross-functional integration of feedback associated with better customer outcomes than channel-siloed data. That places the difficulty in organisational as much as technical territory: unifying signals from surveys, tickets, reviews and behavioural analytics is a precondition for a cross-functional response but does not produce one.

The technical side is well supplied. A mediated schema that reconciles sources never designed to agree is the classical data-integration problem (Lenzerini, 2002), with schema matching (Rahm and Bernstein, 2001), fusion of conflicting values (Bleiholder and Naumann, 2008; Dong and Srivastava, 2015) and identity resolution (Christen, 2012) as its established sub-problems and extract-transform-load as its pipeline form (Vassiliadis, Simitsis and Skiadopoulos, 2002; Kimball and Ross, 2013). Ingestion is hybrid in delivery, since some sources push and others must be polled (Fielding, 2000; Hohpe and Woolf, 2003), and a multi-tenant feedback store must enforce isolation below the application layer and meet the data-quality dimensions surveyed by Batini et al. (2009). Implementing a unified signal schema, as Chapter 4 does, applies this literature; it is not evidence that organisations suffer fragmentation, which rests on the sources above.

### 2.1.4 Routing, workflow and the execution record

Once a signal is normalised, acting on it is a workflow problem. Business process management treats the process model as the central object of design, enactment and analysis (van der Aalst, 2013), and the workflow-pattern catalogue (van der Aalst et al., 2003) names the constructs a feedback lifecycle uses, notably deferred choice, where the next step waits on a judgement not yet made. Statecharts (Harel, 1987) give an insight lifecycle an explicit finite-state form rather than an implicit convention, and Reichert and Weber (2012) show how such workflows accommodate change at runtime. Work distribution to roles and skills is formalised by Kumar, van der Aalst and Verbeek (2002), and the resource patterns of Russell et al. (2005) include delegation, escalation and approval as first-class constructs. Robotic process automation supplies the notion of a rule that may fire unattended, with the flag marking the boundary of delegated authority (van der Aalst, Bichler and Heinzl, 2018; Syed et al., 2020), and Dellermann et al. (2019) argue that hybrid systems must balance algorithmic throughput with human judgement. Two requirements follow for a record that is to serve as evidence rather than telemetry: secure logging with completeness, tamper-evidence and ordering (Accorsi, 2009), and event data of the kind from which process mining can reconstruct actual behaviour (van der Aalst, 2016). Timestamped action logs with status transitions are that event data, which makes an auditable execution record a resource for process improvement as well as for accountability.

### 2.1.5 Practitioner evidence and its commercial interest

Analyst and vendor publications corroborate the gap from practice, with the caveat that every source sells either the diagnosis or a remedy. Gartner's 2026 Magic Quadrant defines the VoC platform function as collecting feedback from multiple sources, analysing it and turning it into prescriptive action, and positions autonomous experience agents as the next wave (Gartner, 2026; CX Today, 2026). The Qualtrics XM Institute's practitioner study finds that most organisations remain at early maturity, collecting data without operationalising it, and names stakeholder inaction and weak linkage to business outcomes as the top obstacles (Mitchell and Quaadgras, 2025). Medallia (2025) reports that closed-loop programmes often fail to deliver the expected impact and attributes this to undefined objectives, the wrong data and a missing connection to operational workflows. Enterpret (2025) markets an agentic feedback platform whose agents detect, route and notify. These sources establish that practitioners report the gap and that vendors are moving from analysis to action; they are not used here to size it. None of them documents, in the sources reviewed, that the actions taken are subsequently measured for effect; where an approval step is documented, as in Enterpret's product documentation, it is not tied to a measured outcome (§6.3 compares named products on dated, cited capabilities).

## 2.2 Enriching feedback under imperfect labels

### 2.2.1 What enrichment must do

Sentiment classification with standard learners outperformed hand-picked indicator words from the start (Pang, Lee and Vaithyanathan, 2002), and the field's taxonomy of opinion mining, subjectivity and polarity (Pang and Lee, 2008) was extended by Liu (2012) to entity and aspect level. That extension is operationally decisive. A single review can praise one aspect and condemn another, and aspect-based sentiment analysis, benchmarked in the SemEval tasks (Pontiki et al., 2014, 2015, 2016) and advanced by attention models (Wang et al., 2016) and BERT-based sentence-pair formulations (Sun, Huang and Qiu, 2019), is the basis for decomposing a signal into themes that carry independent sentiment. Wilson, Wiebe and Hoffmann (2005) showed that contextual polarity often differs from prior polarity, which is why a label set for real feedback needs an explicit mixed category rather than forcing ambivalence into one pole; Chapter 5 reports how the production stage uses that category and what excluding it does to measured agreement. Themes arrive by topic modelling (Blei, Ng and Jordan, 2003; Tirunillai and Tellis, 2014; Grootendorst, 2022) or, in the artifact, through a governed taxonomy. Beyond sentiment, triage needs urgency or risk, and routing on a graded severity scale presupposes that severity can be read from text at all, a claim Chapter 5 examines only as bounded agreement with reference labels of stated provenance, not as a test of the construct. Lexicon methods (Taboada et al., 2011) remain the natural floor for such comparisons, and transformer pre-training (Vaswani et al., 2017; Devlin et al., 2019) with few-shot prompting (Brown et al., 2020) supplies the general-purpose comparator that Chapter 5 measures against them. Where a system attaches a confidence to a label, the calibration literature applies: modern networks are often overconfident (Guo et al., 2017), post-hoc scaling (Platt, 1999) partly corrects this, and in a feedback system the score decides whether a signal routes automatically or waits for a person.

### 2.2.2 Language models as annotators and the problem of the reference standard

Gilardi, Alizadeh and Kubli (2023) found that zero-shot annotation by a large language model exceeded crowd workers on accuracy and intercoder agreement across several text-classification tasks at a fraction of the cost, and Törnberg (2023) reported the same for GPT-4 against experts and crowd workers in a working paper. Hill et al. (2026) compared language-model coding with human analysts in a blinded healthcare study and found deductive coding against a fixed codebook close to human raw agreement while inductive theme generation remained weaker. Their study is an analogy for the artifact's division of labour, in which the model applies a human-governed taxonomy at scale and people govern the taxonomy's evolution (§4.5), and a reason to verify coding rather than a validation of that design: it used an adjudicated reference standard in a low-prevalence setting, and high raw agreement can coexist with low chance-corrected agreement in low-prevalence coding, so raw agreement alone says little about a coder.

Two consequences follow for Chapter 5. The annotation results license a language-model path for triage, which §5A measures against a TF-IDF + logistic regression baseline and a lexicon floor rather than assumes. They also cut the other way for the reference standard. The seed labels of §3.7 were drafted by a language-model assistant, so the risk, journey-stage and owner comparisons are in part comparisons between two models' readings, a same-family bias that the human-origin star rating bounds for sentiment but does not remove elsewhere; neither gold standard is human-labelled. The second rating of 40 risk seeds (κ = 0.55, §3.7) cannot be counted against this bias, because its independent human provenance is unverified. The LLM-as-a-judge literature adds a further caution: model judges show verbosity, position and self-enhancement biases, and panels and calibration are the recommended mitigations (Gu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024). No model judge scored anything Chapter 5 reports, because the adjudication designed in §3.5.2 was not executed.

## 2.3 Interpreting outcome evidence and governing automated action

### 2.3.1 From insight to prescribed action

Business intelligence moved from warehouse and OLAP platforms (Chaudhuri, Dayal and Narasayya, 2011) to self-service (Alpar and Schulz, 2016) and real-time analysis (Sahay and Ranjan, 2008), relocating analysis into the workflow where the work happens; Chen, Chiang and Storey (2012) periodise the field by the data it handles. The data-information-knowledge-wisdom hierarchy (Ackoff, 1989; Rowley, 2007) names the transformations a pipeline performs and supplies Chapter 4's vocabulary for its stages, but it is descriptive: it says which steps exist, not that any system performs them well. Ghasemaghaei, Hassanein and Turel (2017) showed that analytics improve a firm's agility only when the tools fit the tasks and the people using them, so capability alone does not change decisions. Prescriptive analytics goes one step further, from prediction to a recommended action (Delen and Demirkan, 2013; Bertsimas and Kallus, 2020; Lepenioti et al., 2020), and multi-criteria methods weight competing criteria explicitly (Saaty, 1980). A rule layer over inferred insight properties is prescriptive in this sense. It sits at the intersection of the data-driven and knowledge-driven variants of decision support (Gorry and Scott Morton, 1971; Keen and Scott Morton, 1978; Sprague, 1980; Power, 2002; Arnott and Pervan, 2005), with forward-chaining inference (Forgy, 1982; Buchanan and Shortliffe, 1984) evaluating declaratively stated business rules (Ross, 2003; Boyer and Mili, 2011) as properties arrive, so that routing policy is held by the people accountable for it and not by application code. What none of this literature supplies is what happens after the recommendation: its governance, execution and verification lie outside the scope of prescriptive analytics.

### 2.3.2 Governing automated action

Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens (2000) give levels and types of automation. Lee and See (2004) argue that trust should be calibrated on information about the automation's performance, process and purpose, so that reliance matches the automation's actual capability, with disuse and misuse as the two miscalibrations; Glikson and Woolley (2020) extend the evidence to trust in artificial intelligence. The usual resolution is graduated authority: routine decisions execute, consequential ones wait for clearance. Two attentional findings qualify it. Parasuraman and Manzey (2010) show that complacency and automation bias appear in experts as well as novices, grow with workload and are not removed by awareness alone; Dietvorst, Simmons and Massey (2015) show the opposite failure, abandonment of an algorithm after a visible error even when it still outperforms the alternative. An approval design that takes both seriously keeps the decision consequential and the evidence in view, and publishes its measured error rates (DP4, §4.3.1; DP7, §4.4). The meaningful-human-control literature translates the tracking and tracing conditions into system properties an approval gate can be designed and audited against (Siebert et al., 2023), and Laux and Ruschemeier (2025) argue from the automation-bias evidence that the EU AI Act's largely awareness-based Article 14 measures are unlikely to de-bias overseers by themselves, so an oversight design must add friction, justification and auditability beyond the legal minimum. The contemporary agent literature points the same way: surveys of language-model agents catalogue tool use, planning and feedback learning (Li, 2025); work on human-agent collaboration formalises instruction editing and mid-task intervention (Zou et al., 2025); and the HULA agent deployed inside Jira reported reduced effort when engineers steered rather than replaced it (Takerngsaksiri et al., 2024). This is the grounding for the artifact's stance of confining model reasoning to bounded, named stages inside a deterministic, human-checkpointed workflow (Chapter 4). What the gate does and does not do should be stated together: it controls false positives, meaning wrong or harmful actions reaching an external system, and it cannot recover escalation-worthy signals that the enrichment ranks too low (22 of 50 under the seed labels on the production rubric, Chapter 5). A classifier ablation does not compare bounded workflows with autonomous agents, and this thesis makes no such comparison.

### 2.3.3 Knowing whether the action worked

An action taken is not a problem solved. The complaint-handling literature judges closure by the customer's perceived justice and speed: distributive, procedural and interactional justice drive satisfaction with complaint handling and, through it, trust and commitment (Tax, Brown and Chandrashekaran, 1998); which recovery attributes work depends on the type and magnitude of the failure (Smith, Bolton and Wagner, 1999); and the recovery paradox holds only under narrow conditions (Smith and Bolton, 1998). Halperin et al. (2022), in a large-scale field experiment with ride-hailing customers, found that apologies did not restore future spending unless paired with material compensation, and that repeated or promise-based apologies reduced it. Plausible recovery actions can therefore have null or negative effects. That is the empirical core of the outcome-contract decision (Chapter 4) and of DP1, under which closure is graded rather than binary: delivery, target attainment against a contracted threshold over a frozen interval, and attributed improvement from a fitted model, each with its own evidence grade (§4.4), so a ticket done or a reporter told is delivery and no more.

Measuring that movement is the harder problem. Online controlled experiments (Kohavi et al., 2009; Kohavi, Tang and Xu, 2020) are the standard, and their lesson that an accumulated record of measured effects is worth more than any single result carries over to a learning store; but randomisation is infeasible in the single-organisation pilot deployments considered here. The quasi-experimental toolkit fills part of the gap. Segmented regression on an interrupted time series is the minimum credible before-and-after inference for one organisation (Wagner et al., 2002); synthetic difference-in-differences offers a doubly robust estimator for panel settings (Arkhangelsky et al., 2021); and Bernal et al. (2017) set out the caveats, seasonality, autocorrelation and time-varying confounding among them, that keep a fitted model short of a validated causal design. Chapter 3 (§3.5.5) accordingly uses the interrupted-time-series estimate as a read-time estimate shown beside a scheduled checkpoint reading, never as the verdict, and states its refusal below minimum data and its grading there. The organisational-learning cycle makes the same point from the other direction: Plan-Do-Study-Act (Deming, 1986, 1993) and evidence-based management (Pfeffer and Sutton, 2006; Rousseau, 2006) both fail at the Study step when the outcome is assumed rather than measured, and Sterman (2000) treats learning itself as a feedback process for the same reason.

## 2.4 Reusing knowledge under changing conditions

Knowledge management frames the problem as four linked processes, creation, storage and retrieval, transfer and application (Alavi and Leidner, 2001), with externalisation, the codification of tacit knowledge into explicit form (Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995; Polanyi, 1966), as the step a feedback system can support directly: when a trial's outcome is recorded as what worked and what did not, practitioner intuition becomes retrievable rather than personal. Organisational learning adds the distinctions the store must respect. Argyris and Schon (1978) separate single-loop from double-loop learning; March (1991) names the exploration-exploitation tension, which a feedback lifecycle holds on both sides at once, reapplying a validated pattern to a recurring problem while new signals indicate trends no stored pattern covers; Walsh and Ungson (1991) and Argote (2013) treat organisational memory as a structure with retention and retrieval, not a passive archive, and Markus (2001) shows that reuse succeeds or fails with the situation, that is, with who produced the knowledge and who is trying to reuse it. Learning from failure requires that the losing variant be retained beside the winning one (McGrath, 1999), against the systematic under-investment in failure analysis that Cannon and Edmondson (2005) document, and it depends on the psychological safety that lets teams discuss errors at all (Edmondson, 1999). Carlile (2004) locates the boundary problem: an insight is typically produced in one function and actionable in another, so routing across that boundary is where value is realised or lost, and collective performance depends on interaction patterns rather than individual ability (Woolley et al., 2010).

Two strands bear on the decision to treat memory as perishable. Argote, Beckman and Epple (1990) found that production knowledge in wartime shipbuilding depreciated rapidly rather than persisting, and Darr, Argote and Epple (1995) found the same in service franchises, with transfer among commonly owned units and not across owners. Both inferred depreciation from productivity, not from stored learnings, so they ground the shape of DP2, decay and ownership-bounded transfer, but not its rate: §4.3.2 stores a half-life per learning, and the comparison across half-lives is listed in §6.5 as not run. Time-decaying machine memory also has prior art: MemoryBank updates a language model's long-term memory on an Ebbinghaus-style forgetting curve, reinforcing or forgetting by elapsed time and significance (Zhong et al., 2024), and the retrieve-reuse-revise-retain cycle of case-based reasoning (Kolodner, 1993) is being revived inside language-model agent architectures (Hatalis, Christou and Kondapalli, 2025). This bounds the thesis's claim. The contribution is not decay-based memory as such but its application to organisational action-outcome learnings, with a decayed evidence confidence attached to what worked and retrieval under governance into future action decisions rather than conversational recall (§4.3.2). Whether decay changes retrieval appropriately or improves recommendations is an untested hypothesis (§1.7; §6.5).

## 2.5 Design Science Research

Design science seeks to extend organisational capability by building and evaluating purposeful artifacts, where behavioural research seeks to explain and predict phenomena (Hevner et al., 2004). For a thesis whose contribution is a working platform aimed at the gap of §2.1, it is the appropriate paradigm because it treats the artifact as a research output and requires its evaluation against articulated criteria. Hevner et al.'s seven guidelines (a purposeful artifact, problem relevance, design evaluation, research contributions, rigour, design as a search process and communication to both audiences) map onto the thesis as follows. The artifact is Chapter 4. Relevance rests on the process and survey evidence of §2.1 (Wirtz et al., 2010; Homburg and Fürst, 2005; Forrester, 2025b; Fazio et al., 2025) and the recovery evidence of §2.3. Evaluation is the bounded technical and enrichment evaluation of Chapter 5, with the practitioner protocol retained and not conducted at the time of writing. Contributions span a situated implementation and design principles; rigour is grounded in this review; and the search is recorded in §4.7.

Peffers et al. (2007) supply the six-activity process (problem identification and motivation, objectives of a solution, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, communication) with several entry points, of which this thesis takes the problem-centred one. Hevner's (2007) three cycles distinguish relevance (the application environment), rigour (the knowledge base) and design (build and evaluate), and Gregor and Hevner (2013) distinguish levels of contribution: the thesis targets a Level 1 situated implementation with a Level 2 component of nascent design principles. Prat, Comyn-Wattiau and Akoka (2015) taxonomise evaluation criteria by system dimension and by technique, timing and participants, building on the evaluation methods of Hevner et al. (2004); the practitioner protocol (Appendix A) selects perceived usefulness, ease of use, efficacy and organisational fit, operationalised through semi-structured interviews and the System Usability Scale (Brooke, 1996), and is reported as not conducted (§5B.1). The research problem is design-oriented in the sense the paradigm requires: the gap of §2.1 cannot be closed by observation alone but needs an artifact that changes a workflow, and the contribution is dual, a working platform and the design principles abstracted from building and examining it. That is what makes DSR a fit rather than a veneer.

## 2.6 Responsible AI and EU law

As an MSc Responsible AI thesis this work examines its artifact through a governance lens as well as a performance one: routing a complaint, drafting an outreach or flagging a customer has consequences for customers and for staff. The principles now converged in the literature (beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, justice and explicability in Floridi et al., 2018; transparency, fairness, non-maleficence, responsibility and privacy across the guidelines analysed by Jobin, Ienca and Vayena, 2019) translate for a feedback system into transparent rules, auditable action logs, controllable autonomy boundaries and minimal data handling. Human-centred AI rejects the assumed trade-off between human control and automation (Shneiderman, 2020), and the human-AI interaction guidelines of Amershi et al. (2019), among them making clear what the system can do and how well, supporting efficient correction and notifying users of change, bear directly on an approval interface. Rakova et al. (2021) found Responsible-AI practice to be mostly reactive and dependent on structural support, so a platform has to supply the affordances (configurable boundaries, clear defaults, accessible logs) through which practitioners can act responsibly, and not only correct code. Data governance (Abraham, Schneider and vom Brocke, 2019) and the GDPR, with its Article 5 principles, its Article 30 record-keeping and its Article 22 rights over solely automated decisions (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2016), set the data-handling floor; an action log that records the triggering rule, the input signal, the outcome and the responsible party for every action is an explainability mechanism in exactly that sense.

Fairness is a live concern in the component this artifact depends on. Mehrabi et al. (2021) catalogue representation, measurement, aggregation and evaluation biases; Kiritchenko and Mohammad (2018) found statistically significant, consistent score differences by the gender or race signalled in a sentence in several of the many sentiment systems they evaluated; Joshi et al. (2020) document the uneven distribution of NLP resources and performance across languages; and Bender et al. (2021) extend the concern to large language models, where training-distribution skew is inherited rather than authored. Rule-based routing is more inspectable than a classifier but not thereby fair, so routing distributions have to be audited by segment. The choice of metric is itself a design decision: demographic parity is inappropriate where base rates legitimately differ, as severity does by sector and channel, and equality of opportunity (Hardt, Price and Srebro, 2016) instead asks whether signals that genuinely warrant escalation are escalated at the same rate regardless of the language they arrive in, a question about who reaches a human. §5A.5 reports escalation recall by source stratum in this spirit, descriptively only, and states why it ranks no method.

The legal setting is the EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) as amended by the Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744), which postponed the high-risk obligations and kept the Article 50 transparency duties on their 2026 schedule (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024, 2026; the political agreement that preceded the enacted text is recorded in Council of the European Union, 2026, and was read before enactment by Gibson Dunn, 2026, and Latham & Watkins, 2026); Appendix B gives the dates and the article-level analysis. A feedback triage system is unlikely to be high-risk under Annex III (§4.9), so the Chapter III obligations never bound the artifact; the provisions that bear on it are the Article 5 prohibition on manipulative techniques, relevant to automated outreach and retention tactics, the amended Article 4, and the Article 50 transparency duties, of which the provider-side marking of drafted text is the open item. Appendix B analyses these article by article and says for each whether it is an obligation or a commitment. The design stance follows from the classification rather than from any deadline: human oversight, logging and transparency are treated as durable commitments.

## 2.7 The research gap

Each adjacent field supplies one link of the chain and not the chain. VoC and complaint-handling research documents the feedback-to-action gap and the process conditions that narrow it, but stops at diagnosis (Wirtz et al., 2010; Homburg and Fürst, 2005; Forrester, 2025b; Fazio et al., 2025). Prescriptive analytics recommends an action and leaves its governance, execution and verification out of scope (Bertsimas and Kallus, 2020; Lepenioti et al., 2020). Automation theory supplies levels of authority and conditions for meaningful control as general frameworks rather than a situated artifact (Parasuraman et al., 2000; Lee and See, 2004; Siebert et al., 2023). Experimentation and quasi-experimental inference measure whether a metric moved but treat the measurement as a study, not as the closure step of a routing loop (Kohavi, Tang and Xu, 2020; Bernal et al., 2017). Organisational learning establishes that knowledge depreciates and that memory has structure (Argote, Beckman and Epple, 1990; Darr, Argote and Epple, 1995; Argote, 2013; Walsh and Ungson, 1991), and language-model systems now operationalise decaying memory for conversational recall (Zhong et al., 2024), but, in the sources reviewed, not a governed memory of which remedies worked in which contexts; the coverage limit is stated at the end of this section.

| Field | What it provides | Where it stops (in the sources reviewed) |
|---|---|---|
| Voice of the Customer and complaint handling | Documents the feedback-to-action gap and the process conditions that narrow it | No evaluated artifact identified that closes it |
| Prescriptive analytics | Recommends an action | Governance, execution and measured closure out of scope |
| Decision support and automation | Levels of authority; conditions for meaningful human control | General frameworks, not a domain artifact |
| Experimentation and quasi-experimental inference | Measures whether a metric moved | Not tied to feedback routing as a loop step |
| Organisational learning and machine memory | Perishable memory in theory; decaying memory for conversational recall (Zhong et al., 2024) | No decaying, governed store of action outcomes identified |

The gap is stated with the limit of the review's coverage. Within the literature and product documentation reviewed for this chapter (references; §6.3), no evaluated system was identified that combines signal enrichment, governed action under human approval, contracted and graded outcome measurement, and perishable, retrievable learning records in one artifact. The review was narrative and not systematic, so this is a statement about what was found rather than a proof of absence. The closest precedents are the agentic feedback platforms of §2.1.5, which detect, route and notify without, in the sources reviewed, documenting outcome contracts or a decaying learning memory, and the language-model memory systems of §2.4, which decay conversational recall rather than action outcomes. The contribution the thesis claims is accordingly the integration and the design knowledge it yields (Gregor and Hevner, 2013): a working artifact that connects the links under explicit Responsible-AI governance, and an account of the design decisions the integration forces, which the constituent literatures do not resolve (Chapter 4). What the evaluation establishes and what it leaves open is set out in §1.7 and Chapter 7.
